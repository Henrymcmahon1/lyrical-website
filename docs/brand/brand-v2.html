01 · Reconciliation

What we keep, what we adjust, what has to go.

Stated plainly so nobody has to guess which parts of v1.2 are being questioned. Six of the ten items below are adopted unchanged.

Keep

"One song. Any language. Same soul."

Better than anything in v1 of this document. It's short, it's true, and "same soul" is doing the exact work the product needs — it claims fidelity without claiming identity.

Keep

Four colours, four jobs — "not a palette, a division of labour"

This is the best idea in v1.2. Identity, action, space, typography. It's a system rather than a mood, which means it survives contact with a real website.

Keep

Rights-holder infrastructure, not a consumer product

Correct, and it should govern every page. Our own strategy says authorization — not production capability — is the binding constraint.

Keep

Brand voice vs product voice, split across two faces

Genuinely useful principle: if it's the brand talking, one face; if it's the product talking, the other. We keep the rule and question only the two faces chosen.

Keep

Cream as the ground · Ember owns action · solid mark only

Cream confirms a light-ground site, which is our biggest free differentiator. Ember at #EE4E22 is almost exactly the signal orange arrived at independently. And "no recoloured or two-tone mark" rules out gradients — agreed, permanently.

Adjust

Indigo #4433D6 as the identity colour

The problem isn't contrast — it measures 6.8:1 on cream, which passes. The problem is category. Blue-violet in this exact register is the default colour of AI products. Section 04 proposes four alternatives that keep the structure and lose the association.

Adjust

"Two parallel curved strokes"

Parallel and identical means the mark reads as =, not ≈. Our own client material is careful to say transcreation. Section 03 shows the full range so the call can be made on evidence rather than instinct.

Adjust

Sora + Inter

Inter is the single most-used interface font on the web and reads as a default. Sora 200 at text sizes also fails legibility. We keep the two-voice principle and change both faces — see section 05 for why the display face in particular should relate to the mark's own drawing.

Adjust

Stacked lockup as the only sanctioned arrangement

Stacked is the right primary — it's more ceremonial and it suits a cover. But it's too tall for a navigation bar, an email signature or a contract header. We need a sanctioned horizontal utility variant, or people will improvise one badly.

Must go

The 6.4× metric on the applications page

This is not a design objection. Our Monetization Strategy states that native-speaker population figures are "an internal prioritization heuristic" and "should never be presented to an investor as a multiplier on catalog value." Publishing 6.4× as a headline metric contradicts our own written position. Section 07 proposes defensible replacements.

02 · The elegance question

"Like a kid drew it" is a stroke problem, not a shape problem.

The objection is fair and it has a precise cause. A uniform-width stroke with round ends is a felt-tip pen. No amount of adjusting the curve will make a monoline squiggle look sophisticated. What creates elegance is modulation — the stroke swelling at its belly and tapering to a point at each tip, the way a broad-nib pen or a high-contrast serif draws a curve.

v1.2 as specified

Monoline

Uniform 6.4 units, round caps. Friendly, contemporary, and — the criticism is correct — a little like a marker pen. It also has no relationship to any typeface, so the mark always sits slightly apart from the wordmark.

Proposed

Modulated

Same centreline, same geometry, same proportions. The stroke now thickens through the curve and comes to a point. It reads as drawn rather than typed — organic and sophisticated at once, which is precisely the disagreement resolved.

🔍 The consequence is bigger than it looks. A modulated stroke is what a high-contrast serif is made of. Choose that kind of typeface and the mark stops being a logo placed near some type and becomes a character in the same alphabet — the same hand drew both. That's the argument for the typography in section 05, and it's also what makes a subtle asymmetry read as craft instead of error.

03 · Symmetry

Five marks, from perfectly parallel to clearly uneven.

All five are modulated and drawn from identical geometry — the only variable is how much the lower wave departs from the upper, given as a percentage. The question isn't which looks nicest large; it's where refinement tips over into looking like a mistake. My read is that it happens between M04 and M05.

Recommendation: M03 at 6%. At a glance it reads as two matching strokes — so it satisfies the "clean and elegant" requirement completely. Look properly and the lower wave is doing something slightly different, which is the honest claim: same envelope, different signal. M01 is defensible but it means =, and that's the one thing we've been careful never to say.

05 · Typography

Keep the two voices. Change both faces.

The principle from v1.2 stands: if it's the brand talking, one face; if it's the product talking, the other. But the display face should share the mark's construction — that's what makes an identity feel drawn by one hand rather than assembled. All faces below are open source, so no licence cost and no legal exposure on assets that will appear in contracts.

Recommendation: Bodoni Moda + Archivo. A Didone is literally a modulated stroke — thick belly, hairline tips — so it is the same drawing as the mark. That single decision does more for "elegant and sophisticated" than any other choice in this document, and it answers the original objection at the root: nothing set in Bodoni has ever been accused of looking like a child drew it.

06 · Lockup

Stacked stays primary. One utility variant is added.

v1.2 is right that stacked is the better arrangement and should be the sanctioned one. But a stacked lockup cannot fit a 64 px navigation bar or a letterhead rule, and an unsanctioned variant will get invented in a hurry by whoever needs one first. Better to draw it now.
